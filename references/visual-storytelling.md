# From content to visual story

Read this when designing scenes after the user has agreed the flow. These are
options and decision criteria, not a fixed template for every subject.

## Choose a spatial metaphor that belongs to the material

| Material | Useful structure | Demonstration possibilities |
| --- | --- | --- |
| System or workflow | Connected graph / process | Trace one item through operations; show branches, state and output. |
| History or biography | Timeline / places / recurring artefacts | Show changes over time, journeys and influences; preserve chronological and geographic truth. |
| Research or science | Evidence → mechanism → result | Reveal observations, compare conditions, show a mechanism with uncertainty labelled. |
| Business pitch | Customer situation → intervention → outcome | Follow one customer task; demonstrate the product and support commercial claims with actual evidence. |
| Literature or an argument | Excerpt / relationship map / evidence chain | Annotate a passage, connect interpretations to evidence, show alternatives without inventing certainty. |
| Data story | Consistent charts / annotated comparisons | Reveal distributions and changes with stable units, scales and a visible baseline. |
| Portfolio or design process | Evolving artefact / gallery journey | Show source material, constraints, iterations and the resulting work at useful scale. |

Choose a single takeaway for the whole talk. Give each section a distinct job in
building it. Keep the opening anchored in something the audience can picture;
make the conclusion connect the demonstrated parts to the larger implication.
These are prompts for co-design, not a reason to overwrite the user's flow.

## Write a visual contract before coding each scene

Use a short record, adapted to the content:

```text
Purpose: what should the audience understand?
Evidence: which source, example or explicitly illustrative material supports it?
Visible action: what changes on screen, in what order, and why?
Result: what can the audience inspect at rest?
Continuity: what carries into/from the adjacent section?
Framing: what is in view, what must remain hidden, where may the camera travel?
Playback: loop or one-shot, result hold, reset, speaker/navigation cue.
Notes: explanation, caveats, source attribution, handoff.
```

For an order-fulfilment example, a purposeful sequence would show an order,
allocate its stock, route its parcel and update its delivery status. For a biology
lesson, it might reveal a structure, trace a process and display its effect. Each
requires its own representation of meaning.

## Maintain continuity and causality

- Carry the same illustrative object, values, colour meanings and identifiers
  through dependent scenes. Introduce new examples deliberately.
- Ensure outputs appear after the operation that produces them. A dependent
  component should not display a later result while its input is still absent.
- Where alternatives are demonstrated, label or separate them. Avoid showing
  mutually exclusive outcomes as one simultaneous real state.
- For loops, establish the original state, perform the explanation, hold the
  result long enough to understand, then reset clearly. Keep the camera settled
  during most of the explanation. A 6–12 second loop is a useful starting point,
  not a limit on how long someone may speak.
- Combine closely related roles into a scene when this reduces navigation and
  makes their relationship clearer. Keep each role's contribution visible.
- Give chart reveals time for interpretation. Animate actual values rather than
  decorative oscillations, and show uncertainty when the source requires it.
- Avoid premature full-world views. If the plan reserves a reveal, camera bounds
  must enforce it during transitions and on different aspect ratios too.
- If a final reveal is approved, zoom or morph out of the same persistent objects
  into their complete arrangement. A replacement image of an unrelated overview
  loses the connection the reveal should communicate.

## Keep the presentation visually led

Put detail in notes. On screen, retain a useful heading, essential labels, units
and evidence. Check whether someone at the back of the intended room can read
them. A wall of small cards is still text-heavy even if every card is animated.

Preserve diagrams and non-text content that already explain the subject well.
Restructure their framing and reveal order before replacing them. Make a different
visual language for different functions; repeating the same card entrance across
all scenes creates activity without explanation.

Use motion intensity selectively. Gentle spring easing can give controls and
camera changes a responsive feel. Transient blur can soften movement, but it must
clear at rest and should not obscure source evidence, labels or comparisons.
Do not make scientifically false motion merely to increase spectacle.

## Protect factual and creative intent

Distinguish demonstrated capability, simulated behaviour and proposed future
scope. Speaker notes and visuals must agree about what exists. Do not turn a
limited experiment into a universal claim, invent performance figures, or animate
a causal relationship that the source does not establish.

Use supplied/private material only in the agreed destination and form. A public
presentation may need synthetic replacements, crops or anonymisation. Do not
bundle confidential source documents merely because the build can access them.
